Representations of multi-entity systems are commonly probed by their response
to global transformations, not by how a fixed perturbation budget
\emph{redistributed across different coalitions of entities} changes them. We
introduce \emph{support-reallocation probing}: controlled, equal-energy
displacement interventions that redistribute the same displacement operator
over different node supports of a multi-agent scene, turning ``who moves
together'' into a measurable property of a learned representation. Applied to
self-supervised encoders of soccer formations, the instrument shows that
intervention responses are architecture-dependent yet \emph{predictable}: a
normalized local-geometry descriptor---the input-Jacobian Gram of the encoder,
decomposed into per-node and cross-node coupling blocks---ranks intervention
responses with Spearman correlations of $0.5$--$0.85$ across development,
held-out-match, and independent-provider evaluations, within a measured
validity regime ($\varepsilon \lesssim 0.25$ in normalized pitch units) whose
degradation matches the theory's own prediction. The relation is
training-induced (random-initialization responses are five orders of magnitude
smaller), replicates across SSL objective families, exceeds both spectral and
model-free formation baselines, and its informative content shifts from
self-sensitivity to coupling terms as coalition size grows. Guided by this
diagnosis, a task-semantic repair---separating absolute-deployment and
translation-invariant configuration channels with an asymmetric update
allocation---improves all four primary task--geometry metrics on all 8 matches
of an independent data provider (sign test $p<0.01$), at a measured and
selective cost to auxiliary temporal-context retention. These results suggest
that how displacement is distributed over entities is a first-class variable
for multi-agent representation learning, and that local differential geometry
is a practical instrument for measuring, predicting, and repairing it.
