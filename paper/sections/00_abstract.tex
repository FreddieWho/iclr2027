Static correctness does not guarantee reliable updating under change. In a
controlled setting with oracle labels and edits of known effect, we show
that when both atomic edits of a scene are judged correctly, their joint
compositional change is still missed in 151/176 (85.8\%) of fresh-holdout
cases. The same blind spot appears on single semantic-boundary crossings:
28/64 (43.8\%) miss on directly targeted fresh paths, versus 108/128
(84.4\%) of single-turn paths found by a random-edit screen---two rates
from different path populations, reported side by side. Pre-change
confidence separates the two notions of reliability: it predicts
current-state error in the classical direction, yet under true semantic
change the highest-confidence starts are the most likely to update
incorrectly (endpoint error rising from 0.36--0.61 to 0.98--1.00 across
confidence quartiles on six trained models evaluated on dev and
parent-disjoint confirmation banks). Targeted flip coverage improves
component and downstream metrics on the fresh holdout (static error
18.0\%~$\to$~10.7\%; single-flip miss 52.4\%~$\to$~34.7\%; action
invalidity 75.0\%~$\to$~62.5\%), but paired quartet analysis shows most
endpoint gains do not restore full atomic-plus-joint consistency: full
repair is 0.03--0.10 while error migration is 0.36--0.40. Combining
label-free relational input features with the same flip coverage shifts
joint consistency to 0.38--0.42 (dev) and 0.44--0.47 (confirmation), with
full repair exceeding migration on the confirmation bank---a bounded
positive instance, not a general architecture. On real football tracking,
controlled edits and natural turns support transition and repair behavior
but do not reproduce the confidence inversion; the pixel-observation arm
evaluates repair behavior only and does not test confidence. Correctness
under change cannot be summarized by static accuracy or endpoint repair
alone.
